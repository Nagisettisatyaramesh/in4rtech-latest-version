Why most AI pilots stall: it’s an architecture problem

An AI pilot is designed to answer one question: can this work? Scaling it asks a different one: can this work here, every day, with our data, our systems and our controls? That second question is where many promising pilots quietly stop.

The pilot is not the product

Pilots are usually built in a protected space: a clean extract of data, a small group of enthusiastic users and a team that can work around gaps by hand. None of that survives the move into day-to-day operations. The model may be fine; everything around it has not yet been designed.

Where the friction appears

  • Data access and quality. The pilot used a one-off extract. Production needs reliable, governed feeds from systems that were never designed to share.
  • Integration. An AI capability creates value only when it sits inside a real process: a CRM screen, a case-handling workflow, a service desk.
  • Ownership. Who owns the model, the prompts, the data pipeline and the outcome once the pilot team moves on?
  • Governance. Security, privacy and risk teams need to understand how the capability behaves and how it is monitored.

Treat AI as part of the landscape

The organisations that scale AI tend to design for it the same way they design any enterprise capability: with a clear view of the data it needs, the systems it must connect to, the controls it must respect and the people who will run it. That is Enterprise Architecture work, and doing it early turns a successful experiment into something the business can depend on.

A practical starting point is to choose pilots with a visible path to production: use cases where the data already exists, the integration point is known and there is a named business owner waiting for the result.

What an EA review actually delivers in 6 weeks

“Enterprise Architecture review” can sound open-ended. It does not need to be. A well-scoped review is a short, structured piece of work that ends with a shared picture of the landscape and a set of decisions leaders can act on.

A typical six-week shape

Every organisation starts from a different place, so the plan is always tailored. A common pattern looks like this:

  1. Week 1 — Discover. Agree goals and scope, meet stakeholders across business and technology, and gather what already exists.
  2. Weeks 2–3 — Assess. Map applications, data flows, integrations and infrastructure; identify duplication, risk, cost hotspots and technical debt.
  3. Week 4 — Target state. Shape the architecture the organisation is aiming for, and the principles that should guide future decisions.
  4. Week 5 — Roadmap. Sequence the changes into realistic steps, with dependencies, quick wins and the decisions each step requires.
  5. Week 6 — Playback. Walk leaders through the findings and agree what happens next.

What you should expect to hold at the end

  • A clear current-state view of the landscape that people across the organisation recognise.
  • A target architecture and a small set of guiding principles.
  • Rationalisation options: what to invest in, retire, replace or consolidate.
  • A sequenced roadmap, including where AI and automation fit.
  • A short list of decisions for leadership, with the trade-offs explained.

The measure of a good review is simple: technology decisions become easier and more consistent the week after it finishes.

AUTOMATION

Automate the process you need, not the one you have

Automation is most tempting where work is most painful. But the most painful processes are often painful because of how they are designed, and automating them as they stand simply makes the problems run faster.

Look before you automate

Before building a workflow or bot, it is worth asking a few plain questions. Why does this step exist? Where does work wait? Which hand-offs happen only because two systems do not talk to each other? Often the answer changes what should be automated, and sometimes removes the need altogether.

Design, then automate

  • Simplify the process and remove steps that no longer add value.
  • Fix the integration gaps that force people to re-key information.
  • Decide where human judgement genuinely matters, and keep it there.
  • Then automate the repetitive, rules-based work that remains.

Plan for the day after

Automations are software. They need owners, monitoring and a way to change safely as the business changes. A little governance from the start keeps a growing estate of automations reliable rather than fragile.

STRATEGY

Technology roadmaps that survive contact with the business

A roadmap is a promise about the future, and the future rarely co-operates. The roadmaps that stay useful are the ones designed to change.

Anchor on outcomes and decisions

Rather than a long list of projects against dates, a resilient roadmap starts from the business outcomes it supports and makes the key decisions visible: what must be decided, by whom, and by when. Dates follow from that, not the other way round.

Make dependencies explicit

Most delays come from hidden dependencies: a data platform that must exist before an AI use case, or a legacy system that cannot be retired until a process moves. Showing these links makes trade-offs easier to discuss and prevents surprises.

Review it on a rhythm

  • Revisit the roadmap regularly, for example quarterly, with business and technology leaders together.
  • Keep the near term detailed and the longer term deliberately lighter.
  • Treat changes as normal, and record why they were made.

Done this way, a roadmap becomes a working tool for making decisions, not a document that is admired once and then forgotten.
